Lemma 8.13.2 (The reduced boundary and its normal are Borel)713ECED9181E

Lemma 8.13.2 (The reduced boundary and its normal are Borel).713ECED9181E For a locally finite-perimeter set $E$ in $U$, the set $\partial ^*E\cap U$ is Borel, and $x\mapsto \nu _ E(x)$ is Borel on it. In particular it is measurable for every Hausdorff measure. This supplies the measurability needed in the rectifiability statement, starting from [Sim18], equations (4.2)–(4.3), pp. 96–97.

Proof

Put $\mu =|D\chi _ E|$. For each fixed $r>0$, the functions $x\mapsto \mu (B_ r(x))$ and the coordinate functions of $x\mapsto D\chi _ E(B_ r(x))$ are Borel wherever the closed ball lies in $U$. Indeed the kernel $\chi _{\{ |y-x|<r\} }$ is Borel in $(x,y)$, and integration against the positive measure, or the positive and negative parts of each derivative component, preserves measurability. Localize to a countable compact exhaustion of $U$ so all relevant integrals are finite. Thus the normalized vector $A_ r(x)=D\chi _ E(B_ r(x))/\mu (B_ r(x))$ is Borel on the Borel set where its denominator is positive and finite.

On $U\cap \operatorname {supp}\mu $, every sufficiently small denominator is positive. The existence of a limit of $A_ q(x)$ over positive rational $q\downarrow 0$ is a countable Cauchy condition; the assertion that the limiting norm is one is also Borel, using $q=1/k$ once those balls lie in $U$. These conditions are equivalent to the full-radius limit in Definition 8.1.4. To see the nontrivial implication, for each fixed small $r$ take rational $q_ i\uparrow r$. Continuity from below for the finite measure and each signed component gives $A_{q_ i}(x)\to A_ r(x)$. Every uniform small-radius bound on rational averages therefore passes to the real radius $r$. The Cauchy limit over rational radii is consequently the limit over all radii. Intersecting the stated Borel conditions with the relatively closed support gives the Borel reduced boundary. Its outward normal is the negative pointwise limit of the Borel functions $A_{1/k}$, so is Borel there. $\square$
