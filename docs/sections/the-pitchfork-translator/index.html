§ 3.12 The pitchfork translator (Hoffman–Martín–White, 2019)64055D697644

Paper credits and chronology

Title credits include all authors of papers with a reviewed construction of this example, in chronological order. Later analysis, surveys, and citations remain listed here; they do not by themselves earn construction credit. Alternative and numerical constructions are qualified below. These credits are not an exhaustive discovery history. Background-only citations do not determine the date of this example. A classical example may predate the references recorded here.

  1. Hoffman–Ilmanen–Martín–White — 2019-01-25 (first arXiv submission). Survey: Surveys the examples and points to their construction papers. The survey’s full author list is not a construction credit for this example. Passage: Sections 3–5, especially pp. 10–12.
  2. Hoffman–Martín–White — 2019-03-11 (first arXiv submission). Construction: Constructs this family by solving translator boundary-value problems and, where applicable, taking limits and reflecting. Passage: Theorem 2.3 and Section 12.
  3. Gama–Martín–Møller — 2022-09-04 (first arXiv submission). Analysis: Studies entropy, wing counts, and uniqueness of previously constructed translators; this is not the construction source for the family. Passage: Introduction; entropy and pitchfork uniqueness results.
  4. Martín–Sáez–Tsiamis–White — 2024-11-25 (first arXiv submission). Analysis: Completes the semigraphical classification and proves uniqueness of pitchforks and helicoids. Existence of these families is attributed to the earlier Hoffman–Martín–White papers. Passage: Introduction, Theorem 1; Sections 3–4.

The pitchfork is a complete, properly embedded, simply connected, nongraphical translator in $\mathbb R^3$. The supplied notes describe it as a limit of Scherk-type examples, without an explicit formula.

  • It has genus zero. This distinguishes it from Nguyen's periodic infinite-genus tridents despite their similar asymptotic plane arrangements.

  • After reflection to upward translation, it is asymptotic to one plane as $z\to -\infty $ and to three parallel planes as $z\to +\infty $. These are height-direction asymptotics, not a claim that the surface has four topological ends.

  • Its translation yields an eternal noncompact flow. The later construction paper records finite topology and quadratic area growth for its pitchfork subfamily.

See [HIMWNotes19], Section 5 and Figure 7, PDF pages 11–12, and [HMW20]. The existence proof is external. The full construction supplies a pitchfork for each half-width $w\geq \pi $, lying in $|y|<w$. Theorem 1 of [MSTW26] proves uniqueness up to isometry for fixed width in this semigraphical family. Moreover, [GMM25], Theorem 1.5, characterizes pitchforks among complete embedded simply connected translators of finite width, entropy $3$, and containing a vertical line. These hypotheses are essential to the stated classification.

To read the picture, remove the vertical $z$-axis. One component is a graph $z=v(x,y)$ over $\mathbb R\times (0,w)$; the other is its image under $(x,y,z)\mapsto (-x,-y,z)$. In the upward convention the boundary values are

\[ v(x,0)=-\infty \quad (x<0),\qquad v(x,0)=+\infty \quad (x>0),\qquad v(x,w)=+\infty . \]

Theorem 12.1 and Corollary 12.2 of [HMW20] give the smooth extension along the axis, negative Gauss curvature, and the planar and tilted-grim-reaper asymptotics. For $w=\pi $, the grim-reaper profile, after vertical recentering at $y=\pi /2$, is $-\log \sin y$ for $0<y<\pi $. This is an asymptotic profile, not a formula for the pitchfork. The interactive drawing illustrates this width using two colored halves and horizontal sections, following Figures 6 and 14 of that paper. Its connecting geometry is schematic, not a computed solution of the translator equation.
