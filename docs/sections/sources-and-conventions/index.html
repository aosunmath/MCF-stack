1.1 Sources and conventions35E9759B45E2

35E9759B45E2

MCF-stack collects special solutions of mean curvature flow, including self-shrinkers, translators, self-expanders, ancient flows, and other meaningful examples. It aims to connect constructions, geometric properties, and classification results through permanent tags and precise references. This initial edition inherits the mathematical library of GMT-stack, the Stack Project of Geometric Measure Theory and Variational Problems. Its foundational and variational chapters remain as background for the special-solutions collection.

We begin with the common language of geometric measure theory: measures record size, rectifiable sets supply tangent planes, and currents and varifolds encode different geometric data. The inherited exposition connects this language with minimal surfaces, CMC surfaces, min-max theory, phase transitions, obstacle problems, and mean curvature flow. Theorems used without proof are explicitly identified below; their cited proofs remain external dependencies.

Remark 1.1.1 (The source editions).912105D5B712 The initial foundational corpus contains five distinct works or editions. Federer, Geometric Measure Theory (1969), supplies the systematic reference [Fed69]. Simon's Introduction to Geometric Measure Theory, copyright 2017, 2018, is the main source for the initial statements [Sim18]. The supplied 2014 version [Sim14] is retained as a separate edition: its printed page numbers differ from those of the later text.

White's Topics in GMT, with notes by Otis Chodosh dated November 6, 2014, supplies the flat-chain viewpoint [WhiCho14]. The two supplied filenames for these notes are byte-for-byte identical and represent one bibliographic source. Fleming's 2016 article [Fle16] supplies historical context for finite perimeter and integral currents. It is not used as a substitute for a detailed proof of the structure or compactness theorems.

Source citations use the printed page numbers of these editions, not PDF viewer page numbers. In the two Simon files, a PDF page usually contains a two-page spread. The source inventory records file hashes and the page conventions so later additions can use the same editions.

Remark 1.1.2 (Notation and prerequisites).4EDA6AEDEA07 Unless stated otherwise, $U$ is an open subset of $\mathbb {R}^ n$, $1\leq m\leq n$, and $B_ r(x)$ is the open Euclidean ball. We write $\mathcal{L}^ n$ for Lebesgue measure, $\omega _ m=\mathcal{L}^ m(B_1(0))$, and $\mu \llcorner E$ for the restriction of a measure to a measurable set $E$. The notation $W\Subset U$ means that $\overline W$ is compact and contained in $U$. Continuous compactly supported functions form $C_ c(U)$.

We assume elementary Lebesgue integration, the dominated and monotone convergence theorems, and basic differential forms. These prerequisites have not yet been developed in the stack. All almost-everywhere statements specify their measure. Sets of finite perimeter are considered modulo $\mathcal{L}^ n$-null sets. Our normal to a set of finite perimeter points outward; Simon uses the inward normal in Chapter 3, Section 4. For currents we use the comass convention for mass, as explained in Definition 9.1.3.

Remark 1.1.3 (The expanded variational reference).57833B199F67 The first variational additions connect Pitts' book [Pitts81] to minimal hypersurfaces and stability, cycle-space min-max theory, the volume spectrum, Morse index, Willmore geometry, CMC hypersurfaces, curvature networks, and Allen–Cahn theory. They draw on the sixteen additional supplied files, with editions and roles recorded in the source inventory. Those first two batches contained twenty-one distinct works or editions in twenty-two supplied files. The later library expansion adds checked passages on compactness, geometric inequalities, multiplicity, abundance, and flow singularities, together with a larger catalogue of sources awaiting detailed study. The History chapter connects these developments to their original problems and to first-person recollections.

The current foundations include BV compactness, current operations and slicing, and the Plateau existence argument in Theorem 9.4.7. The minimal-surface chapters now connect stability to scalar curvature, the two-dimensional Bernstein theorem, and the spectral criterion for cones. The Allen–Cahn branch proves phase compactness, the perimeter lower bound, energy equipartition, and smooth and interior recovery; its full relative Gamma limit retains the boundary-approximation dependency of Theorem 16.4.7. It also develops Modica gradient bounds, diffuse energy monotonicity, geometric stability, sliding minimization, and bounded monotone-solution rigidity in dimensions two and three, with unique continuation identified as an analytic dependency. The CMC chapter distinguishes total-volume constraints from unrestricted area-minus-volume stability. The mean-curvature-flow chapter develops evolution equations, comparison, Gaussian monotonicity, entropy, rescaling, and the Brakke formulation in Definition 18.6.1. The classical obstacle chapter now includes existence, comparison, reaction measures, quadratic blow-ups, and local proofs of the Weiss and Monneau formulas and uniqueness of singular blow-ups. Its optimal regularity, regular–singular dichotomy, and singular stratification retain external proof dependencies. Free-boundary geometry now includes stationarity, boundary spectra, instability, the equatorial disk, and the critical catenoid. Harmonic Gauss maps and Hopf differentials connect CMC theory to sphere rigidity. Thin obstacles, level-set flows, finer singularity theory, free-boundary existence and regularity, and capillarity require further development. A cited theorem without a local proof remains an external dependency; a chapter in the table of contents does not mean that its subject is complete.

The Examples chapter complements the theory with explicit calculations. Proposition 20.1.10 treats a noninteger-dimensional measure model; Example 20.2.2 and its following results distinguish minimality, completeness, and stability. Theorem 20.4.3 constructs the bowl translator, while Theorem 20.5.3 constructs expanding curves. The ancient oval in Theorem 20.6.1 shows that an ancient flow need not be a soliton.
