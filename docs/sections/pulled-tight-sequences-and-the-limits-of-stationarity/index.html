12.7 Pulled-tight sequences and the limits of stationarity8B18F41E983E

8B18F41E983E

The preceding deformation now acts on whole minimizing sequences. The compactness argument proves stationarity without assuming rectifiability of an arbitrary weak varifold limit. Almost-minimizing selection and replacement regularity remain further steps.

Definition 12.7.1 (A pulled-tight minimizing sequence).FB67EB71A6E8 For a fixed flat-homotopy class $\Pi $ of $\mathbf F$-continuous maps on a finite simplicial complex, a minimizing sequence is called pulled-tight if every member of its critical set is stationary. This is [MNIndex], Definition 2.9. The terminology records stationarity, not smoothness, multiplicity one or index control.

Theorem 12.7.2 (Pull-tight preserves the width and decreases the critical set).CD1277EA8D53 Let $M^{n+1}$ be smooth, closed and connected, $n\geq 1$, and let $\Phi _ i\in \Pi $ be a minimizing sequence in the mod-two boundary-cycle setting of Definition 12.1.2, with finite width $L$. There is a minimizing sequence $\Psi _ i\in \Pi $ such that

\[ \mathcal C(\Psi _ i)\subset \mathcal C(\Phi _ i)\cap \{ V:\delta V=0\} . \]

Moreover $\Psi _ i$ may be chosen uniformly as close to $\Phi _ i$ in $\mathbf F$ as prescribed. This is the continuous pull-tight conclusion of [MNIndex], Section 2.8; the discrete version discussed in [LiAP], Section 4.2, additionally requires interpolation.

Proof

Choose $C>\sup _{i,x}\mathbf M(\Phi _ i(x))$, finite because the maximal masses converge. Use one fixed homotopy from Theorem 12.6.8 and set $\Psi _ i(x)=H(1,\Phi _ i(x))$. The composition is $\mathbf F$-continuous and the homotopy $H(s,\Phi _ i(x))$ is flat-continuous, so $\Psi _ i\in \Pi $. By the definition of the width and mass decrease,

\[ L\leq \max _ x\mathbf M(\Psi _ i(x))\leq \max _ x\mathbf M(\Phi _ i(x))\longrightarrow L. \]

Let $W$ be critical for the new sequence, represented by $|\Psi _{i_ j}(x_ j)|\to W$ with masses tending to $L$. Pass to a subsequence with $V_ j=|\Phi _{i_ j}(x_ j)|\to V$ in $K_ C$. The old masses lie between the new masses and the old maximal masses, so they also tend to $L$. Thus $V\in \mathcal C(\Phi _ i)$ and the mass losses tend to zero. Corollary 12.6.6 gives $V\in S_ C$. Continuity of the varifold deformation then gives

\[ W=\lim _ j\mathscr H(1,V_ j)=\mathscr H(1,V)=V. \]

This proves both stationarity and inclusion in the original critical set. The prescribed closeness follows from the same homotopy, without letting its positive defect vary with $i$. $\square$

Corollary 12.7.3 (Positive width produces a nonzero stationary varifold).CB49E43C32FF Under the hypotheses of Theorem 12.7.2, if $L>0$, there is a nonzero stationary $n$-varifold on $M$ of mass $L$ in the critical set of the original minimizing sequence.

Proof

The pulled-tight sequence has a nonempty critical set by Lemma 12.5.3. Choose a member. Theorem 12.7.2 makes it stationary and a member of the original critical set; Lemma 12.5.3 gives mass $L>0$. This proof yields neither integrality nor regularity. Those stronger conclusions require additional variational selection, as in the external Theorem 12.2.3. $\square$

Proposition 12.7.4 (Near-maximal slices of a pulled-tight sequence approach stationarity).E310F2DBD8F7 For a pulled-tight minimizing sequence with finite width $L$, every $\eta >0$ admits $\delta >0$ and $i_0$ such that

\[ i\geq i_0,\quad \mathbf M(\Phi _ i(x))\geq L-\delta \quad \Longrightarrow \quad d(|\Phi _ i(x)|,S_ C)<\eta , \]

where $C$ bounds all masses. No rate in terms of $L-\mathbf M(\Phi _ i(x))$ is asserted.

Proof

The open $\eta $-neighborhood of $S_ C$ contains the critical set because the sequence is pulled-tight. Apply Lemma 12.5.4 to this neighborhood. The compactness argument supplies qualitative thresholds, with no quantitative lower bound on the auxiliary defect from first variation alone. $\square$

Example 12.7.5 (A diffuse stationary varifold on a compact manifold).D95BB0100791 For $n\geq 1$ on the unit flat torus $M=\mathbb R^{n+1}/\mathbb Z^{n+1}$, let $P$ be the parallel plane spanned by the first $n$ coordinate fields. The varifold defined by

\[ \int f\, dV=\int _ M f(x,P)\, d\operatorname {Vol}_{n+1}(x) \]

is stationary, has positive finite mass, and is not $n$-rectifiable.

Proof

For a smooth periodic vector field $X$, its first variation is $\int _ M\sum _{i=1}^ n\partial _ iX_ i\, dx=0$ by integration over each periodic coordinate. Its weight is ambient volume. Any countably $n$-rectifiable set has zero $(n+1)$-dimensional volume: each bounded piece of a Lipschitz image of a subset of $\mathbb R^ n$ has zero ambient volume by the elementary dimension estimate, and countable unions retain this property. Thus ambient volume cannot be carried by such a set. This example shows why the compact mass cap in the deformation argument cannot silently be treated as a compact space of integral varifolds. $\square$

Example 12.7.6 (Stationary integral varifolds can have crossings).ADB5C190FC3B On the unit flat $(n+1)$-torus, $n\geq 1$, the varifold sum of the coordinate tori $\{ x_1=0\} $ and $\{ x_2=0\} $ is integral and stationary. Its support is not a smooth embedded hypersurface near their intersection.

Proof

Both tori are closed and totally geodesic, hence stationary, and first variation is linear in the varifold. Their intersection has dimension $n-1$ and zero $n$-dimensional measure, so adding them gives an integral varifold with multiplicity one away from the intersection. At an intersection point its tangent cone is the sum of two distinct transverse $n$-planes. The support therefore cannot be locally a single smooth hypersurface, nor a disjoint collection of embedded hypersurfaces. Integrality and stationarity alone cannot replace the almost-minimizing and replacement arguments in Remark 12.2.4. $\square$
