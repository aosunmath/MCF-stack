§ 2.14 Chopp cubical shrinker candidate90FE05D9053E

Paper credits and chronology

Paper credits are chronological and include every author of the cited papers. They identify sources, not exclusive discovery credit. Background-only citations do not determine the date of this example. A classical example may predate the references recorded here.

  1. Chopp — 1994 (publication year).
90FE05D9053E

Chopp's level-set computations produce five new shrinker candidates in [Chopp1994]. These entries describe numerical evidence, not existence theorems. The paper also recovers the Angenent torus of Example 2.13.

04E9A0B8F725

(Numerical genus-five cube) The cubical candidate is a two-dimensional hypersurface in $\mathbb R^3$, with the topology of a closed orientable surface of genus five and cubical symmetry. Chopp starts with the boundary of a ball bored along the three coordinate axes. The connected central cavity has six openings; this is not a genus-three solid with three disjoint tunnels.

The algorithm evolves a family of level sets, selects a level using a geometric ratio, and dilates to maintain a chosen volume. It gives an approximation, with no closed parametrization or exact implicit equation for the shrinker. The target equation and associated flow, in our convention, are

\[ H=\frac{\langle X,\nu \rangle }{2},\qquad M_ t=\sqrt{-t}\, \Sigma ,\qquad -\infty <t<0. \]

Chopp's equation (3.1) instead uses $H=\langle X,\nu \rangle /(2T)$ for an unrescaled surface $S$; the corresponding normalized surface would be $\Sigma =T^{-1/2}S$. His constant-volume display is not automatically the slice at $t=-1$.

The intended surface is smooth, compact, embedded and without boundary; if such a surface exists it is complete and proper and has no ends. The computation does not establish this existence statement. The reported self-similarity diagnostic is $E=0.01869$, and subsequent ordinary MCF approximately preserves the computed shape. This is a finite-grid check, not a certified error bound. No uniqueness, stability spectrum, Gaussian area or entropy value is established for this particular candidate. The diagnostic $E$ is not Gaussian area or entropy.

Source: [Chopp1994], Sections 2–3 and 4.1, pp. 2–8, Figures 8–12. The later constructions in Examples 2.36 and 2.27 have related symmetries; no identification with this numerical surface is asserted. The rotatable illustration is a compact topology schematic with six passages into a cavity, not Chopp's computed mesh or a newly solved shrinker.
